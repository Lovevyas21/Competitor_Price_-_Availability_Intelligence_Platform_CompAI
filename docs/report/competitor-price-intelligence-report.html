Section 1 — Problem Statement

Business context

Retailers and brands lose margin in two ways that are invisible without continuous monitoring. A competitor quietly drops a price and takes volume before anyone notices; or a competitor goes out of stock and the demand that would have gone to them is never captured. Commercial tools such as Prisync, Competera and Minderest sell exactly this capability — competitor price monitoring, repricing signals, and market intelligence.

The problem

Build a system that continuously monitors competitor prices and availability across multiple retailers, records the full history of every change rather than only the latest value, and turns that history into decisions a category manager can act on: who is undercutting us and by how much, which products are volatile enough to need watching, and where prices are likely to move next.

The system must work from public data sources, must not lose history when a source is unavailable, and must be honest about the confidence of what it reports — a price observed three weeks ago should not be presented with the same authority as one observed today.

Expected outcome

A working pipeline that converts raw competitor price observations into a queryable analytical layer, a set of business marts answering specific pricing questions, short-term per-SKU forecasts with measured accuracy, an alerting path for undercutting, and a reporting layer suitable for both technical and non-technical consumers.

Section 3 — Data Sources

The platform is built on Open Prices, the price project of Open Food Facts. It is a public, keyless API carrying crowd-sourced retail prices, each with a real observation date and an OpenStreetMap store location. This matters: one product barcode genuinely appears at several named retailers over time, which is the exact shape a competitor-price platform needs. Product attribute data (name, brand, category) comes from the Open Food Facts catalogue embedded in the same payload.

Section 7 — Data Pipeline

Extraction

Celery Beat enqueues per-source fetch tasks on three cadences: high-volatility SKUs every six hours, mid-tier daily, long-tail weekly. Before any HTTP call, the worker passes two Redis-backed guards — a token bucket for instantaneous rate and a counter for the daily quota. Both live in Redis rather than in the worker, because Celery's own rate limit is per-worker and would silently multiply the request rate as workers scale out.

Every response is written to the raw zone before it is parsed. Failures are separated by kind: a transport error retries with exponential backoff and jitter; a rate limit retries after the limiter's own computed delay; an exhausted daily quota does not retry, because it cannot succeed again until the quota resets, and instead dead-letters immediately.

Transformation

Raw payloads are normalized into a source-independent record, validated per record with Pydantic and per batch with Pandera, then applied to the warehouse with change data capture. Two independent checks catch different failures: per-record validation catches a malformed price, while batch validation catches an entire source returning zero rows or a column that has become all-null after an upstream change.

Change data capture has two implementations with identical semantics. A row-by-row path is the readable reference; a set-based path stages the batch in a temporary table and expresses the same rules in SQL, so cost scales with batch size rather than record count. A parity test runs both over the same dataset and asserts identical output.

Loading

Product attributes are versioned as a Type 2 slowly changing dimension: when a tracked attribute changes, the open row is closed and a new one opened. Price observations are appended only when the price differs from the preceding observation, or when a 24-hour heartbeat has elapsed — which records that the system looked and found no change. History is never updated in place.

Section 8 — Analytics Layer

dbt Core on PostgreSQL, in three layers. Every downstream consumer — API, alerting, forecasting, dashboard and the written brief — reads marts, never the raw event tables. That is what keeps a business rule defined exactly once.

Currency is part of the grain everywhere. The data spans 24 currencies. Subtracting a price in one currency from a price in another produces a number that looks authoritative and means nothing — and would fire false critical alerts, since 12 SEK beside 1.20 EUR reads as a catastrophic undercut when the two are close in value. The gap mart therefore joins on barcode and currency, so a cross-currency pair produces no row rather than a wrong one. Coefficient of variation is used as the volatility measure for the same reason: being unitless, it stays comparable across currencies and price levels.

Section 9 — Forecasting

Per-SKU short-horizon price forecasting using statistical models: AutoETS and AutoARIMA as candidates, with SeasonalNaive as a mandatory baseline. Prices are irregular — an observation appears when someone records it — so each series is resampled onto a daily grid and forward-filled, on the assumption that a shelf price holds until next observed.

Accuracy is measured by rolling-origin cross-validation, not by fit: the model is refitted on successive windows and scored on data it has not seen. Series shorter than a minimum length, or whose most recent observation is older than the staleness threshold, are excluded rather than forecast badly.

All three models score identically, and the naive baseline is never beaten. This is reported rather than hidden: on short, sparse series retail prices behave close to a random walk, and a champion that cannot beat "tomorrow looks like today" is not earning its cost. The pipeline is correct; the data density is the limiting factor. A daily-refresh retail API is what would make forecasting genuinely informative here.

Section 10 — Product Matching

The same physical product appears under different barcodes, titles and languages across retailers. Matching them is what makes comparison possible beyond an exact barcode hit. The policy is deliberately ordered:

  1. Structured identifiers win outright. An exact barcode match is a fact; an embedding similarity is an opinion.
  2. Blocking before vector search. Candidates are restricted by category first, which cuts the comparison space and suppresses false positives on similar names.
  3. Three confidence bands rather than one cutoff.
BandRuleActionPairs
autocosine similarity ≥ 0.92accepted automatically370
review0.80 to 0.92queued for a human decision2,187
reject< 0.80never surfaced—

Embeddings are 384-dimensional, stored in PostgreSQL via pgvector with an HNSW index. A Streamlit screen presents candidate pairs side by side with their similarity score; each verdict is stored with reviewer and timestamp, forming the labelled set against which precision and recall can later be measured.

Alerting

Undercuts are evaluated every six hours. Each alert carries a severity derived from the size of the gap and a confidence label derived from the age of the evidence. Alerts are deduplicated so a persistent undercut is not re-sent every cycle, and alerts resting on evidence older than the freshness threshold are recorded but deliberately not delivered.

Dashboard and written brief

Metabase connects directly to the marts; all dashboard cards were executed against the live database. Separately, a weekly brief is generated from a closed set of facts queried from the marts. An optional LLM narration layer receives the same facts and its output is checked number by number against them; any figure not present in the facts causes the narrated version to be discarded in favour of the deterministic one.

Section 12 — Infrastructure

The target deployment is defined in Terraform: a VPC with the application host in a public subnet and the database in private subnets with no internet route, PostgreSQL on RDS with TLS enforced and no public address, an S3 bucket for the raw zone with versioning and lifecycle tiering, secrets in SSM Parameter Store, a least-privilege instance role, CloudWatch alarms, and a monthly budget alarm. Continuous integration runs linting, the test suites, dbt compilation and Terraform validation without requiring cloud credentials.

Not applied. The configuration passes terraform fmt and terraform validate, which confirms syntax and provider schema only. terraform plan has not been run against a real account, so a first apply should be expected to surface genuine issues such as availability-zone availability, engine version drift and IAM propagation delays.

Section 13 — Testing and Data Quality

The dbt tests assert relationships between fields rather than only per-column constraints: the computed gap must agree with its own inputs, the undercut flag must agree with the gap, maximum must not fall below minimum, and time must move forward within a series. A not_null test would catch none of these; each guards against a refactor silently inverting a rule.

Section 15 — Challenges and Resolutions

ChallengeDiagnosis and resolution
Forecast accuracy looked implausibly good The first run reported 0.15% MAPE. The metric was scoring the forward-fill rather than the forecast: series are resampled onto a daily grid, and on a near-constant series predicting the last value is trivially correct. Scoring was changed to ignore filled days. Every model's reported accuracy became worse, which was the point.
Ingestion throughput collapsed against a remote database The row-by-row CDC path issued roughly five statements per record — acceptable at sub-millisecond latency, pathological at 298 ms. A day's backfill would have taken about 3.3 hours. A set-based implementation reduced it to 41 seconds; both paths were kept, with a parity test asserting they produce identical output.
The warehouse described 2010, not the present The source API returns oldest contributions first by default, so discovery filled the warehouse with genuine but decade-old observations — past every staleness gate, leaving nothing forecastable. Switching the ordering to most-recently-contributed changed the sample from 1 store on 1 date to 22 stores across 11 dates.
Product versions churned on every observation The row-level product name is contributor-entered free text; one barcode carried 25 spellings, opening a new dimension version each time. The canonical catalogue name is used instead, with the free-text field as fallback.
An intermittent test failure A rate-limiter test spent its burst then asserted the next call was refused — which silently depended on four Redis round-trips completing within one second. Under load they did not, a token refilled, and the test failed with nothing wrong. The refill rate was slowed so the assertion no longer races the clock.
Database lost to a storage fault A Docker storage-layer corruption destroyed the volume mid-project. Because every raw payload had been written before parsing, the entire warehouse was rebuilt in 30 seconds with no upstream API calls. This validated the raw-first design under real failure rather than in theory.

Section 16 — Limitations and Future Work

Current limitations

  1. Availability tracking produces no data. No connected source publishes stock levels. The table, partitions and mart are built against the real schema and begin producing numbers as soon as a stock-bearing source is connected.
  2. Forecasting is data-limited. Only 11 products have series dense and recent enough to model, and the naive baseline is not beaten. This reflects the sparsity of crowd-sourced pricing, not a defect in the pipeline.
  3. Cloud infrastructure is unproven. Validated but never applied.
  4. 2,187 match candidates await review. A genuine human workload; raising the review threshold would reduce it at the cost of recall.
  5. Cross-currency comparison is unsupported by design, pending dated exchange rates.

Future work

  1. Connect a keyed retail source, starting with Best Buy. Highest value per unit of effort: it is the simplest approval, it publishes stock — which alone activates availability tracking — and its refresh cadence supplies the dense daily series forecasting currently lacks. Two limitations close together.
  2. Run a plan and apply against a real cloud account, with a budget alarm configured before the first apply.
  3. Work the review queue to a few hundred labelled pairs, then measure precision and recall and tune the threshold on evidence rather than intuition.
  4. Add dated exchange rates to enable cross-currency comparison. The change is additive: a rates table, a converted column in the intermediate layer, and currency-agnostic marts alongside the existing scoped ones.
  5. Move to an orchestrator such as Dagster or Airflow when backfill lineage across interdependent jobs becomes the operational pain point.
